Achilles

Every germ has one spot that never changes. Achilles finds it — then designs the cure that hits it.

Category: Health & Life Sciences Stages: 3 Core tools: AlphaFold · AutoDock · RFdiffusion
01

Problem Statement

A drug doesn't usually fail because scientists got the chemistry wrong. It fails because the germ it was built to fight simply changed — and the weak spot the drug was aimed at isn't there anymore.

1.27Mdeaths directly caused by drug-resistant infections in 2019 — more than malaria
4.95Mdeaths worldwide linked to drug resistance that same year

Source: Global Research on Antimicrobial Resistance (GRAM) Project, published in The Lancet, 2022

Here's the gap. AI tools like AlphaFold can already show us exactly where a germ is weak. Separately, health researchers keep a running record of how real infection samples from around the world are changing over time — basically an early-warning log of new mutations as they show up. Both of these already exist, often in the same public databases. But almost nobody puts them together to ask the one question that matters: is this weak spot still going to be there next year — or has the germ already started changing around it?

That gap shows up in a few concrete ways:

  • Weak spots get picked because they look easy to attack today, not because they're likely to still be there in a year — so a candidate can look perfect on screen and still be outdated by the time it reaches a lab.
  • Finding weak spots, tracking mutations, and designing a treatment are usually three separate jobs, done by three separate teams, on three separate timelines — so nothing catches the mismatch until it's too late.
  • For outbreak-prone places like Bangladesh — recurring Dengue outbreaks, more drug-resistant infections every year — this isn't a small detail. It's the difference between a treatment that still works against the real thing, and one that's already out of date.

Every germ is quietly running its own experiment against every drug we build. This project just asks: why aren't we running that experiment first?

02

Proposed Solution

A single three-stage pipeline — not three separate tools — that finds a target, tests it against real mutation data, and only then designs something built to survive what comes next.

STAGE 01

Find the Target

Pull protein sequences of a priority germ from public databases like UniProt. Use AlphaFold / ColabFold to predict 3D structure and locate a druggable binding pocket using fpocket, then screen public small-molecule libraries from PubChem against it using AutoDock Vina to shortlist candidates by binding affinity (kcal/mol).

NCBI Virus · UniProt · AlphaFold · fpocket · PubChem · AutoDock Vina
STAGE 02

Test It Against Real Mutations

Pull real-world mutation records for the same germ from CARD and BV-BRC/PATRIC (for drug-resistant bacteria) or NCBI Virus Variation and GISAID (for Dengue). Score how much each position in the pocket varies across real samples using a conservation metric (Shannon entropy) — a low score means the spot has stayed the same across many samples, a high score means it's already shifting. AlphaFold is used only to confirm the pocket's geometry is still intact on the mutated structure, not to score how damaging each mutation is. Together this produces a live map of which targets are still reliable, and which are already being dodged.

CARD · BV-BRC/PATRIC · NCBI Virus Variation · GISAID · Shannon entropy · AlphaFold (structure check)
STAGE 03

Design Something That Survives It

For every pocket confirmed as conserved in Stage 02, pull prior-art records from the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB), the Protein Data Bank (PDB), and the AMP-specific databases APD3 and DBAASP to establish what has already been attempted against this pocket class. Stage 03 then runs two parallel design tracks simultaneously — one anchored to 3D pocket geometry, one anchored to antimicrobial peptide biology — so that the final shortlist is both structurally precise and biologically potent. Both tracks feed into a single convergence gate before anything is forwarded for wet-lab consideration.

Track A — Structure

Pocket-Anchored Binder (RFdiffusion)

Use RFdiffusion to generate de-novo backbone scaffolds conditioned on the exact conserved pocket coordinates from Stage 02 — producing candidate binder shapes fitted to the target geometry rather than a generic template. Feed those backbones into ProteinMPNN to recover amino-acid sequences that fold stably onto each scaffold. Score binding free energy (ΔΔG, kcal/mol) with Rosetta FastRelax, keeping only candidates below −1.5 kcal/mol, then filter for solubility and aggregation risk via CamSol. Best suited for viral targets and well-defined deep pockets where structural lock-and-key fit is the primary requirement.

RFdiffusion · ProteinMPNN · Rosetta FastRelax (ΔΔG) · CamSol

Track B — AMP

Antimicrobial Peptide (AMP Diffusion)

Use an AMP-specific latent diffusion model (e.g. Diff-AMP or AMPGen) operating in the ESM-2 protein language model embedding space, conditioned on the pocket's epitope sequence extracted in Stage 01. This generates short peptide candidates (15–40 aa) with cationic, amphipathic character proven to disrupt bacterial membranes. Score antimicrobial probability with APEX (≥ 0.8 threshold), remove hemolytic candidates with ToxGIN (score < 0.5), and apply the same CamSol solubility gate. Best suited for bacterial targets such as drug-resistant A. baumannii where membrane disruption adds a second mechanism independent of pocket binding.

APD3 · DBAASP · Diff-AMP / AMPGen (ESM-2) · APEX · ToxGIN · CamSol
⇣

Convergence Gate — AlphaFold-Multimer

All survivors from both tracks are fed into AlphaFold-Multimer to model the full binder–target complex. A candidate passes only when (1) the interface confidence score is ipTM ≥ 0.7, and (2) ≥ 60 % of the predicted interface contacts land on the conserved residues that Stage 02 confirmed are not drifting — not on the variable flanking regions. This shared gate ensures that whether a candidate arrived via Track A or Track B, it is verified to engage the same mutation-resistant anchor. The output is a single ranked shortlist — each entry carrying a backbone or sequence, a ΔΔG or AMP score, and a confirmed interface map — ready for prioritisation before any wet-lab commitment is made.

IEDB · PDB · AlphaFold-Multimer · ipTM ≥ 0.7 · conserved-residue interface check ≥ 60 %

04

Demonstration Scope

To stay achievable and complete rather than broad and shallow, the prototype will select one germ and one protein target — for example a resistance-related protein in drug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, or a Dengue surface protein — and carry it fully through all three stages. Additional germs are framed as future work, not part of the initial demo.
